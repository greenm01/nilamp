● The answer is no — the W-function was not extended to tubes. Here's why, with
  evidence:

  Evidence

  The 27-page PAK213 TriJmos paper has zero mentions of "Lambert", "W-function",
   or "W(...)" — I grepped the full extracted text. It's purely a SPICE
  simulation model:

  - B1 source converts the VDMOS square-law to a programmable power-law for 
  triodes (line 36)
  - Set m=1.5 since SITs are usually a 3/2 power law (line 860)
  - The model uses lookup tables (X1, B3) for Island effect and per-curve
  fitting

  Compare with the project outline: every Level-1 section (PAK101–118) is
  explicitly W-function work on exponential I-V devices (diodes, BJTs). PAK213
  sits in Level 2 and is titled "Basic Triode Tube modelling", not "Triode
  W-function solution". There's no PAK section for tube push-pull, tube Lin
  topology, or tube DoubleCross — Level 4 is entirely BJT/MOSFET.

  Why this makes sense

  The Lambert W is the natural tool for circuits with exponential I-V laws — I =
   Is·exp(V/Vt) plus a linear element gives y·e^y = x, solved by W. Triodes
  follow a power-law (Child–Langmuir 3/2): Ia ≈ K·(Vg + Va/μ)^m with m≈1.5. A
  power-law plus a linear load resistor is solvable by ordinary algebra (or
  Cardano for the cubic when m=3/2) — no transcendental W needed.

  So Ian's tube work is a parallel track: instead of a closed-form W-function
  solution, he built TriJmos, a generic LTspice subcircuit on top of the VDMOS
  that fits triodes, SITs, and jFETs with table-driven power-law curves. The
  motivation in the paper is filling gaps in Koren's and Duncan's existing tube
  SPICE models (low Vₐ, positive Vg, smooth derivatives), not extending the
  W-function framework.

  Bottom line

  - Tube SPICE modelling: yes, well-developed (TriJmos, 17 worked examples,
  LSK389B/12AX7/12AT7/12AU7 fits).
  - Tube W-function analytical solutions: no, and probably by design — the
  device physics doesn't call for it.

  If you wanted to take this further, the open question would be: is there a
  tube circuit topology where a W-style closed-form would be valuable (e.g., a
  hybrid stage where a tube drives a BJT)? Want me to look at the LTspice
  schematics in the PAK213 zip to see if Ian explored any hybrid topologies?

❯ would this work be applicable to real-time dsp for simulating tube amps? how  
  does the large-signal analysis compare to Wave Digital Filters (WDF) or nodal 
   DK-method solvers?                                                           

● Short answer: partially applicable, but not as a drop-in. Ian's work is
  fundamentally a DC large-signal analytical technique, while WDF and DK-method
  are dynamic real-time simulation frameworks. They live at different levels of
  the stack — PAK could feed into a WDF/DK pipeline, but it doesn't replace one.

  Where PAK is useful for real-time tube DSP

  Closed-form nonlinear solves. WDF and DK both spend most of their per-sample
  CPU inside Newton-Raphson iterations resolving nonlinear elements. For
  diode-clipper / BJT stages, the Lambert W gives a direct closed form — and
  W(e^x) is cheap (Veberič or Fukushima rational approximations land in ~10–20
  ns). Several diode-clipper plugins already do this (Holters, Parker,
  D'Angelo). PAK's contribution is extending that idea to richer topologies:
  Banwell's CE with shunt feedback (PAK104), CE with emitter degeneration
  (PAK105), differential pairs, etc. So when a WDF "root" or DK nonlinear block
  reduces to one of Ian's solved topologies, you can substitute his explicit
  formula and skip the inner iteration entirely.

  Aliasing. PAK114 gives dW(eˣ)/dx and d²W(eˣ)/dx² in closed form, and PAK118
  derives HD2/HD3 analytically. Smooth, exact derivatives are exactly what
  antiderivative antialiasing (ADAA) wants. WDF/DK aliasing is normally fought
  with oversampling; closed-form derivatives let you do ADAA cleanly on the
  static nonlinearity inside the solver.

  Smooth Anlauf model. The SoftPlus-style anode-current model in TriJmos
  translates straight to DSP — it's a log(1+exp(·)) waveshaper, well-behaved
  under ADAA.

  Where it doesn't help

  Tubes themselves. Triode I-V is a power-law ((Vg + Va/μ)^m), not exponential —
   that's why Ian's tube model is a SPICE subcircuit with lookup tables, not a
  W-formula. For real-time you'd typically use a parametric closed-form (Koren,
  Cardarilli, or Dempwolf's piecewise) rather than table interpolation. TriJmos
  was designed for accuracy in LTspice, not for cycle-budget DSP.

  Reactive elements and topology composition. PAK is per-stage DC. Real tube
  amps live or die by inter-stage coupling caps, Miller capacitance, cathode
  bypass time constants, output transformer hysteresis, and PSU sag. Ian flags
  this himself — Level 5 PAK502 ("DDE W-stability... can it be used for
  approximate AC analysis?") is explicitly open research. WDF and DK have
  native, principled handling of energy-storage elements via
  bilinear/trapezoidal discretization. PAK does not.

  Multi-nonlinearity coupling. Modern WDFs use R-type adaptors (Werner) or
  multidimensional Newton (MDN-WDF) to resolve coupled nonlinearities; DK does
  it via the joint Jacobian. PAK solves specific topologies one at a time and
  doesn't compose — you can't bolt two PAK stages together and get a closed form
   for the system.

  Comparison frame

  - DK-method: most general, handles any topology, slowest per sample (one
  Newton solve per timestep over all NL devices). Tools: ACME.jl, Holters'
  papers.
  - WDF: faster when topology fits a tree/R-tree, requires more cleverness for
  complex circuits. Tools: Werner's RT-WDF, Chowdhury's chowdsp_wdf.
  - PAK / W-function: fastest when applicable because there's no iteration at
  all — but applicability is narrow (specific exponential-device topologies) and
   it only gives the static map, not the dynamics.

  How I'd actually use it

  Build the amp in a DK or WDF framework for the dynamics, but identify the
  nonlinear sub-blocks that match Ian's solved topologies (CE shunt-feedback
  stage → PAK104; LTP → PAK304; current mirror → PAK305) and swap the iterative
  solver inside those blocks for his closed-form. You get the WDF/DK system
  structure plus PAK's iteration-free inner kernel. For the actual tube stages,
  use Koren or similar — PAK doesn't add value there.

● Pulled the canonical PAK form from the User Guide. Here's the assessment per
  topology.

  PAK102 — diode + series resistor

  Id(Vin) = (N·Vt/Rs) · W( exp((Vin - Vk) / (N·Vt)) ) - Is
  Vk      = N·Vt · ln(N·Vt / (Is·Rs)) - Is·Rs

  Drops in cleanly. This is the standard diode-clipper WDF root (Holters/Parker
  form, also Werner). At a WDF nonlinear root the rest of the circuit is
  presented as Thévenin (a, Rp), which is exactly Ian's (Vin, Rs). Crucially, Rp
   is fixed at WDF adapt time, so Vk is precomputed once — per-sample cost is
  one W(exp(·)) plus a multiply. No iteration.

  PAK105 — CE BJT with emitter degeneration + base resistance

  Ic = (N·Vt/Req) · W( exp((Vin - Vk)/(N·Vt)) ) - Is
  Req = Rin/β + Re·(1+β)/β            ← all R referred to collector side
  Vk  = N·Vt·ln(N·Vt/(Is·Req)) - Is·Req

  Drops in cleanly with one caveat. The clever trick is referring Rin and Re to
  the collector side and lumping into a single Req, after which the topology is
  structurally identical to PAK102. So in WDF this becomes a one-port at the
  collector node with Rp = Req (β-folded), and the base-side and emitter-side
  Thévenin ports are absorbed into Req and the input shift Vin. Same precompute
  story.

  Caveat: this is the basic model — no Early effect, no Vce saturation, β
  constant. Once you turn on PAK207–211 (Vce sat, β-fall, Early,
  electro-thermal), Ic becomes a function of Vce and Tj too, which couples a
  second port back in. Ian handles those with multi-diode extensions that don't
  reduce to a single W call. For real-time you'd either accept the basic model
  or fall back to Newton at that node.

  PAK204 — push-pull CE (V+D+R)//(-V+D+R)

  This is two of the above in parallel with opposite-polarity bias. It reduces
  to PAK112/113 generalized solutions, not pure Lambert W. Still closed-form,
  but evaluation is more expensive (multi-step or single multi-arg
  approximation). For WDF it's still a one-port at the output node — workable,
  but you'd benchmark whether one PAK113 evaluation beats one Newton iteration.
  Probably yes if you tabulate, probably break-even if you don't.

  PAK304 — BJT differential pair (LTP)

  Tail current splits between two transistors per Vid = Vbe1 - Vbe2. This
  collapses to tanh(Vid/2Vt)-style closed form for the ideal LTP — well-known,
  and already used in WDF input-stage models. PAK304 adds finite tail impedance
  and base/emitter R, which keeps it closed-form but messier. Drops in.

  What doesn't fit

  - PAK207, 209, 211 (Vce sat, Early, thermal): introduce a second port
  coupling. No single-W reduction.
  - PAK408+ (3-stage Lin topology, full power amp DC): too many coupled
  nonlinear stages — Ian solves them as a system offline, not as a sample-rate
  map.
  - PAK213 / TriJmos triode: power-law + table, no W. For tubes in WDF you'd use
   Koren-style closed form (Yeh's WDF triode does this) or a black-box
  (WaveNet-style). PAK adds nothing here.
  - Output transformer: not in PAK at all. This is usually the dominant
  nonlinearity in a tube power amp tone — you need a separate hysteresis model
  (Jiles-Atherton or similar) regardless of which framework you use.

  Practical recipe for a tube preamp emulation

  1. Tube stages: Koren in a WDF root (or DK with cached Newton).
  2. BJT/jFET buffer & follower stages around the tubes: substitute PAK105 /
  PAK106 for the inner Newton in WDF — one W(exp(·)) per sample, no convergence
  checks, smooth derivatives for ADAA.
  3. Diode clipping (effects-loop diode pairs, grid-stopper protection): PAK102
  form, already standard.
  4. Coupling caps, cathode bypass, Miller: handled by the WDF/DK framework's
  bilinear discretization — no help from PAK.
  5. Output transformer: separate problem, separate model.

  The honest summary: PAK is most useful as a closed-form replacement for the 
  Newton kernel inside specific nonlinear roots of an otherwise-standard WDF or
  DK topology. The framework around it stays the same.

❯ would this improve tube amp simulation in regard to LCR components and        
  BJT/MOSFET stages?                                                            

● Splitting honestly by component class.

  LCR components — no direct improvement

  PAK has nothing to say about reactive elements. It's a DC large-signal
  analysis tool — Ian explicitly flags AC analysis as open research (PAK502:
  "DDE W-stability... can it be used for approximate AC analysis?"). Capacitors,
   inductors, transformers, RC time constants — all of that stays inside
  whatever WDF/DK framework you wrap around it, discretized via
  bilinear/trapezoidal as usual.

  There's one indirect benefit worth naming: trapezoidal discretization of an
  LCR network containing a diode/BJT yields, at each sample, an algebraic
  constraint of the form I_nl(V) = G·V + V_inj where (G, V_inj) is the
  per-sample Thévenin from the linearized LCR state. Standard practice is to
  solve this with Newton inside the per-sample loop. With PAK, that constraint
  has a closed-form solution — the same W(exp(·)) form, just with a
  per-sample-varying Thévenin. So you can run bilinear integration of the LCR
  network without an inner Newton. That's a robustness and predictable-cost win,
   not a model-fidelity win. The L and C still behave exactly as the framework's
   discretization makes them behave.

  BJT stages — concrete improvement

  This is the strongest case for PAK. Per nonlinear node:

  - Speed: one W(exp(x)) via Veberič/Fukushima rational approximation lands
  ~10–30 ns. Newton on the same node typically takes 3–5 iterations of similar
  per-step cost plus convergence checks. 3–5× per-node speedup, with
  deterministic cost.
  - Robustness: no NR convergence failures at high-gain operating points (near
  cutoff, near saturation). Eliminates a known source of audio glitching in
  WDF/DK tube emulations.
  - Aliasing: smooth, analytic dW/dx and d²W/dx² (PAK114) mean ADAA applies
  cleanly to the static map. Reduces required oversampling for clean clipping.
  - Topologies covered: CE+Re+Rin (PAK105), CC follower (PAK106), LTP (PAK304),
  current mirror (PAK305), cascode (PAK309), Rush/composite pair (PAK311). Most
  BJT cells you'd find in a tube amp's solid-state support circuitry.

  Caveat: PAK's basic forms ignore Vce saturation, Early effect, and β-fall.
  Once you need those (PAK207–211), the closed form breaks — you fall back to
  Newton or piecewise. For preamp-level signals away from the rails, the basic
  form is fine.

  MOSFET stages — marginal

  MOSFETs are square-law (Id ∝ (Vgs - Vth)²), not exponential. A
  MOSFET-plus-resistor circuit reduces to a quadratic — closed form via the
  standard formula, no W needed. PAK212 documents Ian's MOSFET treatment but
  doesn't unlock anything you didn't already have algebraically. Real-time
  MOSFET emulation is already cheap; PAK adds no meaningful speedup. Use 
  BSIM-lite or simple square-law-plus-subthreshold; PAK is overkill here.

  (jFET stages: Ian treats these via TriJmos, so they inherit the triode-style
  table-and-power-law approach, not a clean W form. Same caveats as tubes.)

  Bottom line for tube amp realism

  A tube amp's tone is dominated by (1) output transformer 
  saturation/hysteresis, (2) PSU sag, (3) tube nonlinearity, (4) grid-blocking 
  on coupling caps. PAK helps with none of those. It helps with the auxiliary
  BJT stages around the tubes — effects-loop drivers, cathode-follower SS
  replacements, input buffers, Mesa-style FX-loop interfaces. Those are real
  components in real amps, but they're rarely the dominant tone shapers.

  Net: PAK is a useful per-node optimization for the BJT parts of a hybrid tube
  amp model. It's not a step-change in realism — the output transformer and tube
   models still set the ceiling on quality. Treat it as a CPU/robustness win on
  the BJT cells, not as a new approach to tube emulation.

❯ are large signal solvers not useful for tube stages?                          

● Good push — my framing was too narrow. Let me correct it.

  Large-signal closed-form solvers are useful for tube stages. The reason PAK
  doesn't reach them isn't that the idea fails for tubes — it's that PAK's
  specific math toolkit (Lambert W) is shaped around exponential I-V, while
  tubes are power-law. There's a parallel toolkit for tubes that delivers the
  same per-sample-no-Newton benefits.

  The math, briefly

  Pure 3/2 power-law triode + plate resistor. Take Ia = K·(Vg + Va/μ)^(3/2) with
   Va = Vcc - Ia·Ra. Substitute and let y = Vg + Va/μ:

  y + (K·Ra/μ)·y^(3/2) = Vg + Vcc/μ

  Square and rearrange — you get a cubic in y, solvable by Cardano in closed 
  form. One-shot per sample, no iteration. Same speed/robustness/ADAA story as
  PAK105 for BJT, just polynomial instead of transcendental.

  Koren-style smooth triode. Koren's model has a variable exponent and sqrt(Kvb 
  + Va²) smoothing, so it's not pure power-law and doesn't reduce to Cardano.
  But Cohen & Hélie (IRCAM, 2010–11) showed you can reformulate Koren-class
  triodes with a SoftPlus / log(1+exp(·)) regularization in the cutoff region,
  and the resulting algebraic constraint is solvable by Lambert W — single W
  call per sample, same as a diode clipper. Dempwolf and others have published
  similar W-reductions for tube waveshapers.

  Pentode. Adds the screen grid → another nonlinearity. Cohen-Hélie extend the
  same W-trick; or you can split it into two coupled W-solves.

  So the philosophy of PAK — closed-form large-signal map at each nonlinear
  node, evaluated once per sample — transfers cleanly to tubes. It just uses
  different special functions (cubic roots and/or Lambert W with a softplus
  reformulation).

  Where Ian gets close but doesn't push through

  The TriJmos model already uses SoftPlus for the Anlauf region (log(1+exp(·))
  to give the tube a smooth low-Va knee). SoftPlus and W(exp(·)) are closely
  related shapes — you're a reformulation away from a W-solvable triode. Ian
  uses SoftPlus inside a SPICE table-driven model for accuracy in LTspice; he
  never crosses the bridge to a closed-form per-sample solver because that's not
   his project's goal. The framework is there, but PAK213 stops at modelling,
  not analytical solution.

  Practical implication for tube amp DSP

  ┌────────────────────┬────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┐
  │      Approach      │  Per-sample    │             Accuracy              │
  │                    │      cost      │                                   │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Pure 3/2 + Cardano │ 1 cubic root   │ Decent for clean stages, off in   │
  │                    │                │ cutoff                            │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Cohen-Hélie W-form │ 1 W(exp(·))    │ Good across the operating range   │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Koren + Newton     │ 3–5 NR iters   │ High                              │
  │ (Yeh)              │                │                                   │
  ├────────────────────┼────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────┤
  │ ANN / WaveNet      │ 1 forward pass │ Very high but black-box           │
  └────────────────────┴────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘

  Closed-form large-signal solvers (rows 1–2) are absolutely viable for tubes —
  they sit alongside PAK's BJT solvers in the same architectural slot inside a
  WDF/DK frame: "replace the inner Newton at this nonlinear node with a direct
  evaluation."

  Revised bottom line

  For a tube preamp emulation built on WDF/DK:

  - Tube stages: use Cohen-Hélie W-form (or Cardano for plain 3/2). Same
  per-sample win as PAK gives for BJT — closed-form, no convergence checks,
  smooth derivatives. PAK itself doesn't supply the formula but the idea is
  identical.
  - BJT/jFET stages around the tubes: PAK105/106/304 directly.
  - LCR: WDF/DK framework's bilinear handling, unchanged.
  - OPT, PSU sag: separate models.

  So the right way to read PAK isn't "BJT-only technique that doesn't
  generalize," but "one instance of a broader closed-form-large-signal-solver
  pattern, with the tube counterpart living in the Cohen-Hélie / Cardano corner
  of the literature." Both ends of that pattern are useful for tube amp DSP.

❯ so in theory large signal analysis could potentially give real time tube amp  
  simulation?                                                                   

● Not just theory — it's already shipping. Real-time tube amp simulation using
  large-signal analytical solvers has been published since ~2009 and is in
  production code today.

  What's been demonstrated

  - Yeh's Stanford dissertation (2009): full Fender Bassman and Vox AC30
  emulation at 44.1 kHz on commodity hardware using DK-method with Newton at the
   nonlinear roots.
  - Cohen & Hélie (IRCAM, 2010–11): closed-form Lambert-W triode/pentode
  reformulation, real-time at audio rates.
  - Werner's R-type WDF dissertation (2016): multi-nonlinearity tube circuits in
   real-time WDF.
  - Eichas, Möller, Zölzer (2014–17): fully analytical state-space tube preamp
  models.
  - Open source today: Jatin Chowdhury's chowdsp_wdf library (used in several
  commercial plugins), ACME.jl for DK, RT-WDF.
  - Production plugins: Universal Audio's tube amps, IK Multimedia's AmpliTube
  tube models, several boutique guitar plugins all use some flavor of
  analytical/numerical tube modeling.

  CPU budget sanity check

  At 48 kHz, you have ~20 µs per sample on one core of a modern CPU. Typical
  per-sample costs:

  ┌──────────────────────────────┬───────────┬──────────┐
  │          Operation           │  Cycles   │ At 4 GHz │
  ├──────────────────────────────┼───────────┼──────────┤
  │ Lambert W (Veberič rational) │ ~50–100   │ ~25 ns   │
  ├──────────────────────────────┼───────────┼──────────┤
  │ Cardano cubic                │ ~100–200  │ ~50 ns   │
  ├──────────────────────────────┼───────────┼──────────┤
  │ Newton on BJT (3–5 iters)    │ ~600–2500 │ ~600 ns  │
  ├──────────────────────────────┼───────────┼──────────┤
  │ WDF adaptor update           │ ~20–50    │ ~12 ns   │
  └──────────────────────────────┴───────────┴──────────┘

  A complete Plexi-class amp (8–9 nonlinear stages + ~30 LCR elements + tone
  stack + feedback) lands around 5–15 µs per sample with closed-form solvers —
  comfortable real-time with multi-channel headroom.

  What closed-form actually buys

  It's not "makes real-time possible" — Newton-based DK was already real-time in
   2009. It's:

  - 3–5× lower per-sample CPU at nonlinear nodes → more channels, lower
  oversampling, better battery life on mobile.
  - Deterministic worst-case cost → no audio glitches from NR convergence
  failures at high-gain operating points (the failure mode that haunts
  Newton-based tube emulators when driven hard).
  - Clean ADAA via analytical derivatives → less reliance on 4×/8× oversampling,
   which dominates the actual CPU bill in production plugins.

  The genuinely hard parts that remain

  These don't get easier from any solver choice:

  - Output transformer hysteresis (Jiles-Atherton or Preisach): this is a
  stateful nonlinearity. Analytical static maps don't help — it's memory, not
  algebra. Often approximated linearly or with simplified hysteresis, which is
  why many plugins sound "almost right."
  - PSU sag: dynamic compression coupled across the whole amp at sub-audio
  rates. Usually faked with an envelope follower.
  - Speaker + cab impedance interaction: nonlinear coupling between OPT and
  reactive load. Often offloaded to a separate IR convolution.
  - Microphonics, heater hum, tube aging: usually ignored.

  Frontier trade-off

  The competitive landscape today is roughly:

  - Analytical (PAK-style closed-form, Cohen-Hélie tubes, WDF): physically
  interpretable, parameter-tweakable, deterministic. Quality ceiling set by your
   model accuracy.
  - Numerical (DK + Newton, iterative WDF): same physics, slower, occasional
  glitches under hard drive.
  - Black-box ML (WaveNet, LSTM, GRU as in NeuralDSP, Tone3000, etc.): highest
  measured fidelity to a specific amp, very low per-sample cost, but no
  parameter knobs and no insight into why it sounds the way it does.

  The interesting research direction right now is hybrid: analytical for the
  structurally well-understood parts (tube stages, BJT cells, RC networks) and
  ML for the parts that resist clean modeling (OPT hysteresis, speaker
  interaction). That's where I'd point a project today.

  So yes — real-time tube amp sim with large-signal analytical solvers is
  solved-in-principle and delivered-in-practice. The frontier moved on to OPT,
  PSU, and learned components.

● Neural Amp Modeler (NAM) by Steve Atkinson is the most popular open-source
  entry in the ML-based amp emulation track I mentioned. Here's how it works.

  What it does

  NAM is a black-box system identifier. You give it paired audio:

  - Input: a known signal (DI guitar, or a "reamp" test signal) playing into the
   amp
  - Output: what the amp produced (mic'd cab, or line out)

  It trains a neural network — currently a WaveNet-style causal dilated 
  convolutional network, sometimes LSTM variants — to learn the function
  output[n] = f(input[n], input[n-1], ..., input[n-k]). After ~3 minutes of
  training data and 30 min–few hours of training (on a GPU), you get a .nam
  file: a few hundred KB to ~1 MB of weights.

  The plugin loads that file and at inference time it's just a forward pass
  through the network for each sample. Modern variants (Lite/Feather/Nano
  configs) run comfortably real-time on a laptop CPU.

  Architecturally, what it actually models

  The network has no knowledge of tubes, transformers, RC networks, or any 
  circuit topology. It just learns the input→output map of whatever was on the
  other end of the cable. That includes:

  - Tube nonlinearity (preamp + power amp)
  - Output transformer saturation/hysteresis
  - PSU sag dynamics (within the network's receptive field, ~tens of ms)
  - Bias point, grid-blocking, all the messy stuff

  So it captures the system-level behavior that analytical methods struggle with
   (OPT hysteresis, sag) for free, because it never tries to understand them —
  it just fits.

  The cabinet/speaker is usually handled separately via a linear convolution IR,
   because cabs are linear-ish and IRs are cheap. Some captures bake the cab in;
   the convention is "amp-only" capture + separate IR.

● Your split is in the right spirit, but the naive chained version has a problem
   worth understanding. Then I'll go through what each method actually captures.

  Why naive chaining doesn't fully work

  A real tube amp isn't a feedforward chain preamp → OPT → speaker. There are
  coupling paths your hybrid would miss:

  - Negative feedback loop: most tube amps tap a small voltage off the OPT
  secondary and feed it back to the phase inverter cathode (or earlier). That
  couples output behavior into the front-end transfer function.
  - Power tube loading: the OPT's primary inductance and reflected speaker
  impedance load the power tubes, changing their operating point. The OPT isn't
  just "downstream of" the power stage — it's part of the power stage's
  small-signal and large-signal behavior.
  - PSU sag: heavy signal pulls current through the rectifier, dropping the B+
  rail, which changes bias on every tube simultaneously. This couples the output
   power back to every gain stage's operating point.
  - Cathode bypass interaction with sag: cathode caps that decouple at audio
  rates re-couple at sub-audio rates as the rail moves.

  So if you just train an ML model on "OPT input → speaker output" and bolt it
  after an analytical preamp+power stage, you've severed these loops. The hybrid
   will sound plausible but won't reproduce the touch-sensitivity and "feel"
  that experienced players notice.

  The architecturally correct hybrid

  The cleaner pattern is analytical circuit topology with learned component 
  equations — sometimes called gray-box or differentiable DSP:

  - The circuit (nodes, mesh equations, feedback loops) is built analytically as
   a WDF or DK system.
  - Specific component equations — the tube I-V curve, the OPT B-H curve, the
  speaker impedance — are replaced with neural networks or learned parameter
  sets.
  - Everything is solved jointly per sample, so coupling and feedback are
  preserved by construction.
  - Training is done end-to-end: you reamp through the real amp, and gradients
  flow through the differentiable circuit solver back into the learned component
   models.

  This is research-stage but real. Engel et al. (DDSP, 2020), Wright & Välimäki,
   Damskägg, Esqueda — there's a growing body of work on this for guitar amps,
  synthesizers, and effects. The Aalto group and Queen Mary's C4DM are active.
  It's harder to train than NAM (need a differentiable ODE/circuit solver) but
  you keep working knobs and the system-level coupling.

  The genuinely hard things

  A few phenomena resist all current methods:

  this; you re-bias / re-capture instead.

   output, but at high levels it depends on enclosure pressure and speaker
  excursion, which depend on cone position history — stateful, hard.

  enough ML to train it.

● That's a sharp observation and largely correct. The list reads like a
  designer's enemies list:

  - Microphonics: actively fought with tube dampers, careful chassis layout,
  microphonic-grade tube selection.
  - Thermal drift: fought with bias servos, matched tube pairs, thermal
  compensation.
  - Tube aging: a maintenance problem, not a design feature — you replace tubes
  when bias drifts too far.
  - Hum: fought with twisted pair heater wiring, DC heaters in high-gain stages,
   hum balance pots, star grounding.

  If a designer succeeded perfectly, none of these would exist, and a perfect
  model of the intended circuit would sound exactly like the real thing. The
  fact that they leak through is engineering failure, not engineering choice.

  So the right framing is: modeling effort should track design intent. What the
  designer tried to make happen (tube saturation curves, OPT saturation, sag,
  feedback loop dynamics, RC tone-shaping) is worth modeling carefully. What the
   designer fought against (the items above) is generally worth ignoring or
  modeling with cheap stochastic approximations.

  The complicating wrinkle

  The boundary isn't always clean, because some "imperfections" became
  character:

  - PSU sag is technically a regulation failure, but it's beloved — Class A
  combos sag in a way solid-state rectified amps don't, and players prize it.
  It's modelable (and good plugins do).
  - Slight microphonic regeneration in cranked high-gain amps contributes to the
   "alive" feel and sustain. Some players claim to hear when it's missing.
  - Heater hum bleed in vintage Fenders is part of the genre's idle sound.
  Plugin users sometimes complain modern emulations are "too quiet."
  - Output transformer phase shift and bandwidth limits are imperfections that
  became part of the tone signature.

  So a more precise version of your point: the truly non-musical imperfections
  (aging, drift, microphonics in moderation, hum) are not worth modeling. The
  musical imperfections (sag, transformer behavior, output stage breakup)
  absolutely are — and current methods do model them, with varying success.

  Implication for DSP

  This actually argues that the gap between digital and analog amps is smaller 
  than the audiophile discourse suggests. Once you've modeled:
  - Tube nonlinearities (analytical or learned)
  - OPT saturation/hysteresis (the genuinely hard part)
  - PSU sag dynamics
  - Coupling cap and feedback loop behavior
  - Speaker nonlinearity

  …you've captured everything the designer designed. What's left is noise floor
  and aging — and those are either undesirable, easily synthesized
  stochastically, or both.

  The "warmth" people credit to imperfection is mostly designable behavior they
  don't realize is designable. That's a more interesting frontier than chasing
  microphonics.

❯ so WDF could be improved with PAK? The hybrid method you mentioned?           

● Yes, and that's the most concrete near-term win on the table. Two distinct
  levels of "hybrid" worth separating:

  Level 1: PAK closed-form roots inside WDF (engineering integration, not 
  research)

  Standard WDF tree → at each nonlinear leaf node, the rest of the circuit
  reduces to a Thévenin (a, Rp) where Rp is fixed at adapt time. The leaf needs
  b = f(a, Rp). For:

  - Diode: PAK102 W-form, already standard practice (Holters, Parker, Werner).
  - BJT + Re + Rin (CE/CC): PAK105/106 W-form. Same shape as the diode root,
  just with Req = Rin/β + Re·(1+β)/β substituted. The β-folding trick is the
  contribution — once you do it, the BJT-with-degeneration is a one-port
  nonlinearity from the WDF tree's perspective.
  - LTP / differential pair: PAK304 reduces to a tanh-like closed form, also
  workable.
  - Tube triode/pentode: not PAK — use Cohen-Hélie's W-reformulation or pure
  Cardano on a 3/2 model. Same architectural slot.

  Vk and Req are precomputed once per topology adaptation, so the per-sample
  cost is one W(exp(·)) plus a few multiplies. No Newton, no convergence checks,
   deterministic latency, smooth analytical derivatives for ADAA. That's the
  win.

  This isn't research — the components are all published. What's missing is a
  clean library that exposes these PAK-derived roots as drop-in WDF
  nonlinearities. chowdsp_wdf has diode and tube roots already; nobody has
  shipped a comprehensive Banwell-extended set covering BJT-with-Re, LTP,
  current mirror, cascode in one place. That's a genuine "go build something"
  opportunity if you want a project.

  Level 2: PAK + WDF + learned components (research frontier)

  The gray-box DDSP direction. PAK's role here is the same — analytical
  closed-form at the BJT/jFET nodes, freeing CPU and gradient-flow budget for
  the learned components (OPT B-H, speaker nonlinearity) elsewhere in the
  circuit. WDF's structure is preserved; learned blocks slot in where physics is
   intractable.

  This is research-stage, harder to build, but more interesting if you want to
  push the field rather than productize what already works.

  Practical caveats for Level 1

  - Variable knobs: pots that change Rin or Re change Req change Vk — you have
  to recompute when the knob moves. Cheap (it's a log and a couple of
  multiplies), but you need to wire it up. R-type adaptors handle this
  naturally.
  - Multi-port nonlinearities: PAK works when the BJT collector reduces to a
  single port to the rest of the circuit. If you have direct coupling between
  two BJTs (Sziklai pair, Darlington with shared loading) you may need an R-type
   adaptor with a 2D Newton, or use Ian's PAK301/302 multi-transistor solutions
  if they exist in closed form.
  - Early effect / Vce sat: PAK basic forms assume Ic independent of Vce. Once
  you need PAK207–211 corrections, you lose the single-W form. For preamp
  signals far from the rails, basic is fine.

  So yes — WDF + PAK roots is a real, near-term improvement. You'd be combining
  a 2009-era simulation framework with a 1971-era analytical technique (Banwell)
   extended by Ian. Not glamorous, but it's the cleanest path from "interesting
  math in a PDF" to "actual DSP code that runs."

● It's none of the methods we've been discussing — it's a fourth approach: a
  block-diagram / Hammerstein-style model with lookup-table waveshapers and
  analytical small-signal expressions. Pragmatic, CPU-frugal, optimized for
  fanless tablets. Here's the breakdown.

  Core method: parameterized waveshaper + linear filter chain

  Static nonlinearity: A generalized logistic function (GLF) — a parameterized
  family glf(x, kbias, b, type, kloop) that produces asymmetric soft-clipping
  shapes by curve-fitting. It's not Koren, not Cohen-Hélie, not Lambert W. It's
  a flexible sigmoid family the author chose because it produces
  realistic-looking tube transfer curves with three intuitive parameters.

  Antialiasing: ADAA (Parker/Esqueda/Bilbao 2016 — antiderivative
  anti-aliasing). Implemented via a 1500-segment lookup table storing polynomial
   coefficients of both the function and its antiderivative. A second-order IIR
  equalizer compensates for the implicit lowpass that ADAA introduces. He
  benchmarks it as ~12 dB better than no oversampling, ~5 dB better than 2×
  oversampling, ~3 dB worse than 4×.

  Local feedback (e.g., un-bypassed cathode): GLF embedded in a feedback loop.
  Notes Newton-Raphson could solve this per-sample but deliberately avoids it:
  pre-computes the closed-loop function by resampling the lookup table at design
   time. No per-sample iteration anywhere.

  Tube stage architecture

  Each topology (common cathode, cathode follower, cathodyne phase splitter,
  long-tail pair) is a block diagram:

  1. Pre-gain k_pre (from analytical small-signal mu/R formulas)
  2. GLF waveshaper with kbias, b, kloop
  3. Bias-shift dynamics via first-order lowpass on cathode voltage (models
  cathode bypass cap behavior under signal)
  4. Output gain / impedance scaling

  The small-signal coefficients are pure analytical formulas — closed-form
  expressions in tube μ, Ra, Rk, RL. Standard textbook tube amp small-signal
  analysis. The nonlinear part is all in the GLF.

  This is essentially a Wiener-Hammerstein structure: linear → memoryless
  nonlinearity → linear, with a slow dynamic feedback (cathode bias) wrapping
  the nonlinearity.

  Other components

  - PSU sag: explicitly modeled as RC ladder filters with current-draw feedback
  into the rail. This is more sophisticated than most plugins.
  - OPT: not modeled as a transformer at all — its effect is folded into the
  speaker filter chain.
  - Speaker: linear model derived from Thiele-Small parameters (resonance peak
  EQ + inductance high-shelf). Two filter stages bracketing the power tubes
  interpolate between small-signal output impedance (~80Ω) and saturated output
  impedance (~2.5Ω) to capture the loading-changes-with-saturation effect.
  Clever simplification.
  - Tone stack: "universal SVF" — three-band state-variable filter, NOT the
  literal 5E3 RC network. Predictable response, decoupled bands. Sacrifices
  authenticity for usability.
  - Implementation: JSFX (Cockos's scripting language for REAPER), runs in the
  free YSFX plugin.

  Honest assessment

  This is a deliberate simplification approach. The author isn't trying to model
   the 5E3 from first principles — he's identifying which behaviors audibly 
  matter and modeling only those, with the cheapest math that captures them:

  - Static tube nonlinearity → GLF + ADAA ✓ (good)
  - Cathode bias dynamics → first-order LPF on bias ✓ (decent)
  - PSU sag → RC ladder with load coupling ✓ (good — better than most)
  - Speaker resonance + treble lift → Thiele-Small filter ✓ (good)
  - OPT hysteresis → ignored ✗
  - Real tone stack → replaced with cleaner SVF ✗ (deliberate)

  It's the practical engineer's approach, not the academic's. He's not chasing
  publishable novelty — he's chasing "sounds like a Tweed Deluxe, runs on a
  Surface Pro 7." Comparing to our prior discussion: he sidesteps the WDF/DK/PAK
   question entirely by noting that for this circuit family, you can get away
  with curve-fit waveshapers + linear small-signal expressions and nobody will
  hear the difference.

  If you wanted to compare directly: a WDF + Cohen-Hélie tube + Jiles-Atherton
  OPT model would be more physically accurate, more flexible across amp
  circuits, but 3–5× more CPU and significantly more code. Keller traded those
  things for the tablet target.

  Worth reading the JSFX source if you want a clean, compact reference for "how

● That's a genuinely good synthesis idea. The two approaches are complementary,
  and the right way to combine them is more interesting than just swapping
  pieces. Let me sketch what it actually looks like.

  The clean integration: PAK generates Keller's tables

  Keller's runtime is built around lookup tables for two reasons: speed and ADAA
   compatibility (he needs both f(x) and F(x) = ∫f per sample). His 1500-segment
   polynomial table is genuinely fast — replacing it with per-sample W(exp(·))
  evaluation costs you roughly 3–5× more CPU.

  But you don't have to replace it at runtime. You replace what generates it:

  1. At design time, take physical tube parameters (μ, Kp, Ex, Vct from Koren's
  model, or Cohen-Hélie's reformulation) plus the surrounding circuit (Ra, Rk,
  RL).
  2. Solve the closed-form Ia(Vg) over the input range — analytical (Cardano for
   3/2 power-law, Lambert W for Cohen-Hélie's softplus reformulation, or one
  offline Newton for Koren).
  3. Compute the antiderivative analytically — both W(eˣ) and (Vg + 
  offset)^(3/2) have closed-form integrals (W²/2 + W + C and (2/5)·(...)^(5/2)
  respectively).
  4. Sample both the function and antiderivative into Keller's 1500-segment
  table format.
  5. Fit the polynomial coefficients per segment as he does.

  Result: same runtime cost as Keller, but the table is generated from physics
  rather than curve-fit. ADAA still works exactly as he wrote it.

  What you replace vs. keep

  Replace (with PAK-style physical model):
  - The GLF waveshaper itself — the table now represents a real tube curve at a
  real operating point, not a curve-fit family.
  - The kbias / b / kloop parameter exposure — these become derived quantities
  from physical tube + circuit parameters, not user-tunable shape knobs.
  - Per-tube/per-circuit retuning — adding a 6V6 vs 6L6 vs EL34 vs KT88 is now a
   parameter swap, not a curve-refit.

  Keep (Keller's contributions are independent of the NL model):
  - Block-diagram architecture (Wiener-Hammerstein per stage)
  - ADAA + IIR equalizer
  - Cathode bias dynamics via first-order LPF — physics-correct already, no
  replacement needed
  - Lookup-table-resampling trick for local feedback (cathode follower) — works
  the same way with a physical NL inside the loop, just done offline
  - PSU sag RC ladder with load coupling — Keller does this better than most, no
   improvement available from PAK
  - Thiele-Small speaker filter — independent of NL model
  - Universal SVF tone stack — independent of NL model
  - JSFX/YSFX runtime — keep

  The audible difference is modest. The dominant factors in tube amp tone (sag,
  OPT/speaker interaction, gain staging, tone stack) are all in Keller's
  framework already and unaffected. You're improving the part of the chain
  that's currently curve-fit, which is mostly second-order perceptually.

  The bigger win is architectural: a plugin built this way is a platform, not a

   tube types, circuit values, topology selection — rather than a fresh round of
   curve-fitting.

  Honest scope

  A few subtleties to be aware of:

  - Power amp Class AB crossover is where physical models pay off most — the GLF
   asymmetry is hard to tune correctly across the full Vg range, but PAK-style
  closed forms get the crossover region right by construction. If you only do
  this for one stage, do it for the power amp.
  - Pentode vs triode: 5E3 power tubes are 6V6 pentodes. Cohen-Hélie published a
   pentode reformulation with two W-evaluations (one for plate, one for screen).
   Worth using rather than approximating with a triode.
  - OPT: still not addressed by either Keller or PAK. If you wanted to push
  further, this is the remaining frontier — Jiles-Atherton or Preisach
  hysteresis, or a learned model. Probably more impact on the final sound than
  the tube curve refinement.
  - Cathode follower in a feedback loop: Keller's table-resample trick works
  fine with a physical NL inside, but the offline Newton convergence has to be
  solid across the whole input range. Easy enough.

  So: yes, this is a real and tractable project. It's not glamorous research but
   it would result in a more flexible, more physically grounded tube amp plugin
  without giving up Keller's CPU advantages. If you actually wanted to build
  something for fun, this is a reasonable scope — it's well-defined, the math is
   all published, and the existence of Keller's source as a starting point cuts
  a lot of the implementation risk.
